I built a small QA pipeline for hand-held demonstration data and ran it over the public UMI "cup in the wild" dataset: {{hours}} hours of people doing a task with a hand-held gripper and a wrist camera. This page shows what it caught, the frames that prove it, how I tested the checker itself, and how it carries over to glove data.

Timestamps are exactly 0.1 s apart, frame indices never skip, there are no missing values, the video index agrees with the data for {{meta_ok}} of {{episodes}} episodes, and every episode I decoded has exactly one frame per row. 2. A hand that walked 14 metres

POSE_RUNAWAY

Episode 125 starts normally, then the recorded position slides away for 36 seconds: {{path125}} m of travel inside a {{ext125}} m box, ending {{end_dist}} m from where it began and {{drop}} m lower. The camera stays over the same kitchen table the whole time.

{{chart125}}

Frames at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of episode 125. Same table, same cups. Median recorded hand speed is {{speed125}} m/s, while the camera's median image motion is {{flow125}} px. A typical episode's median is {{flow_typ}} px.

No per-frame speed check fires on this episode, because the runaway is smooth. It is caught by a whole-episode check: on this task, 99.9% of episodes stay inside a {{p999}} m box. My first version of this check used a standard deviation and missed exactly this kind of slow drift. My own validation caught that (section 6).

3. Same room, different origin

FRAME_OFFSET

Fifteen episodes move normally, but their centre sits {{off_lo}} to {{off_hi}} m from the centre of the whole dataset. They come in runs of neighbouring episodes. Episodes 381 to 389 were recorded in the same conference room, at the same table, as their unflagged neighbours 377, 379, 390 and 391. The coordinate origin moved, not the person.

This is harmless for a policy that only learns relative motion, so these episodes pass with a warning instead of quarantine. It still costs every model trained on absolute state, because they are {{frac}}% of frames:

4. {{naive_wraps}} rotation spikes that are not real

ROTVEC_WRAP

Hand orientation is stored as an axis-angle vector, which wraps around at ±π. A naive check that subtracts consecutive vectors finds {{naive_wraps}} "spikes" of more than 1 rad per frame. Measuring the true angle between consecutive rotations finds {{true_spikes}} steps faster than 10 rad/s anywhere in the dataset. Every one of those spikes is the representation wrapping, not the hand moving. Quarantining them would throw away good data, so the checker records them as information only.

5. Where the failures happen

Five of the six jump episodes are in the videos I downloaded, and all five were recorded outdoors: three at a sunny patio (136, 140, 144) and two at a busy sidewalk café (165, 170). Indoor episodes nearby pass, and so do some outdoor ones, so outdoors looks like a risk factor rather than a verdict. My guess is that camera-based tracking struggles with strong sunlight and moving people. That is a hypothesis, not a measured cause.

One frame from each of episodes 130 to 180. Flagged: 136, 140, 144, 165, 170.

The operational point: failure rates should be tracked per location, per station and per device, not only per episode. A glove that starts failing shows up as a cluster long before anyone looks at a single episode.

Camera–hand sync

The camera is mounted on the hand, so what it sees is dominated by the hand turning, not by it sliding sideways. Aligning optical flow against rotation speed gives a median peak correlation of {{corr_rot}}, against {{corr_tr}} for translation. Over {{sync_n}} episodes with a trusted estimate, camera and pose line up at a median of {{sync_med}} ms (5th to 95th percentile: {{sync_p5}} to {{sync_p95}} ms). That is what you would expect here, since UMI computes the pose from this same camera.

{{chart_sync}}

To check the estimator, I shifted the pose stream against the video by ±1, 2, 3 and 5 frames in {{sync_trials}} trials. It recovered the planted shift with a mean error of {{sync_mae_ms}} ms, and {{sync_half}}% of trials landed within half a frame. None of the decoded episodes is out of sync, and the check is able to tell.

On a glove, the gyroscope measures rotation speed directly. The same alignment, gyroscope against camera optical flow, estimates the IMU–camera offset for every episode without extra hardware.

The first run found a real gap: slow drift was caught in only 1 of 200 episodes, because a standard deviation hides a smooth ramp. I replaced it with a whole-episode extent check using a threshold taken from the data. It now catches 200 of 200, and nothing else changed.

What comes out

Every episode gets a passport: a content hash, metrics, and findings with reason codes and frame numbers. The release manifest lists what is included and what is quarantined, and it carries its own hash, so "v14 is worse than v13" becomes a diff, not a guess.

What I would do with glove data

Camera, IMU, force in lockstep

Per-episode offsets from gyroscope vs optical flow, and from force onset vs the moment contact becomes visible, stored as numbers with thresholds, not assumptions.

Dead or drifting sensors

Frozen frames by hash, stuck or saturated channels, force zero-drift per session, and a missing channel treated as missing, never as zero.

Physics before training

Hand speed, extent and rotation limits like the ones above, plus finger joint limits and bone-length consistency, and retargeting feasibility for each robot.

Per glove, per station, per day

Failure rates by device and location, so a bad glove is found from a cluster in the first hour, not from a model that trains badly a week later.

Limits

  • One public dataset at 10 fps. Video was decoded for 3 of its 15 files ({{files}}): {{n_video_eps}} episodes, about {{pct_video}}%. The pose and metadata checks ran on all {{episodes}}.
  • The likely causes (tracking relocalisation outdoors, a moving map origin) are hypotheses read from the data and frames, not confirmed with the dataset's authors. The findings are about this LeRobot copy of the dataset.
  • Thresholds suit a tabletop task with a hand-held gripper. A new task or device needs its own thresholds, taken from its own data the same way.
  • It has no force or IMU channels, so those checks are a plan here, not a result.
